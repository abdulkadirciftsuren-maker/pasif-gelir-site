Behind your morning coffee lies a story of **more than 500 years**: from the mountains of Ethiopia to the monasteries of Yemen, from **Istanbul's coffeehouses** to the cafés of Vienna and Paris. Here's how coffee spread around the world, and the **big role the Ottoman Empire** played along the way.

> **Short answer:** The coffee plant's home is **Ethiopia**. It was first drunk as a beverage by **Sufis in Yemen** in the 15th century. In **1554, Istanbul's** first coffeehouses opened and quickly became the heart of city life. In the 17th century coffee reached Europe through **Venice, London, Paris and Vienna**.

## The legend: Kaldi the goatherd

The best-known legend tells of an Ethiopian goatherd named **Kaldi** who noticed his goats dancing around into the night after eating red berries from a shrub. He took the berries to a monk, and the monks used the drink to stay awake during night prayers.

It's a lovely story, but **a legend**: it was first written down in the 17th century, long after coffee was discovered. What's certain is that the **Arabica** plant grows wild in Ethiopia's highlands. More on where coffee grows: [coffee growing regions](/en/coffee-growing-regions/).

## Yemen: from monasteries to ports

The first reliable records of coffee as a **drink** come from **Sufi monasteries in Yemen in the mid-15th century**. Dervishes drank it to stay awake during night devotions.

The Yemeni port of **Mocha (al-Mukha)** was the world's most important coffee port for centuries. Even today, "mocha" is linked with coffee.

In the early 16th century coffee reached **Mecca** and **Cairo**. In 1511 the governor of Mecca tried to ban it; the ban was soon lifted. It was one of coffee's **first bans**, but not the last.

## Istanbul: the first coffeehouses

According to the chronicler **Peçevi**, Istanbul's first coffeehouse opened in **1554 in the Tahtakale district**, run by two Syrians, **Hakem** from Aleppo and **Şems** from Damascus. Coffeehouses quickly became popular:

- People **talked**, played **backgammon and chess** and recited **poetry**.
- **Meddah** storytellers performed, and **Karagöz** shadow plays were staged.
- They became so loved that people called them **"schools of the wise"** (mekteb-i irfan).

Coffee found its place at the palace too: the palace kitchen had a **chief coffee maker** (kahvecibaşı), and brewing coffee was considered a craft of its own.

## Bans and debates

Once coffeehouses became places where people **talked politics**, rulers grew nervous. Scholars also debated whether coffee was permissible.

The harshest ban came under **Sultan Murad IV** (1623–1640): coffeehouses were closed, coffee and tobacco banned and offenders punished severely. Yet no ban lasted; coffeehouses reopened every time.

## Turkish coffee: a way of brewing

**Turkish coffee** isn't actually a type of coffee but a **brewing method**: powder-fine coffee is slowly heated with water (and sugar, if you like) in a **cezve** and served **with its grounds**. It comes with water and often Turkish delight.

- The saying "**A cup of coffee is remembered for forty years**" shows coffee's place in hospitality.
- In the traditional marriage proposal visit, coffee still plays a role.
- **Coffee cup reading** is a fun part of this culture. See [Turkish coffee fortune telling](/en/turkish-coffee-fortune-telling/).
- **Turkish coffee culture and tradition** was added to **UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage** list in 2013.

Recipe: [how to make Turkish coffee](/en/how-to-make-turkish-coffee/). Regional specialties: [dibek coffee](/en/dibek-coffee/) and [menengiç coffee](/en/menengic-coffee/).

## Coffee reaches Europe

In the 17th century, coffee spread through cities that traded with the Ottoman Empire:

- **Venice:** With strong trade links to the Ottomans, Venice was one of coffee's gateways to Europe. Its first coffee shops are generally dated to around 1645.
- **England:** The first coffeehouses opened in **Oxford** in 1650 and **London** in 1652. They were called "**penny universities**": for a penny you got coffee, newspapers and debate. The insurer **Lloyd's** was born in a coffeehouse.
- **Paris:** In 1669 the Ottoman envoy **Süleyman Ağa** served coffee at the court of Louis XIV, and coffee became fashionable in Paris. **Café Procope** opened in 1686 and is still open today.
- **Vienna:** After the **Siege of Vienna in 1683**, there's a famous story about coffee sacks left behind by the Ottoman army. But Vienna's first official coffeehouse license went to the Armenian merchant **Johannes Diodato** in 1685. Viennese coffeehouse culture is now on Austria's UNESCO intangible heritage list.
- **Germany:** The first coffeehouses opened in **Bremen** and **Hamburg** in the 1670s. Leipzig's "**Zum Arabischen Coffe Baum**" is one of Europe's oldest coffeehouses still in business, and **Bach** wrote his famous **"Coffee Cantata"** in Leipzig in the 1730s.

## Coffee goes global

Europeans wanted not only to drink coffee but to **grow** it:

- The **Dutch** began growing coffee on **Java** (Indonesia) in the late 17th century.
- In the 18th century coffee reached the **Caribbean** and **South America**. **Brazil** became the world's largest producer, and still is.
- **Robusta** was discovered in Central Africa in the late 19th century and spread because it's hardy. See [arabica vs robusta](/en/arabica-vs-robusta/).

## Why does Turkey drink more tea today?

In the late Ottoman period and the early years of the Republic, wars and economic hardship made **coffee imports expensive and difficult**. At the same time, **tea growing in Rize** was promoted, and tea spread as a cheap, homegrown drink. Today tea is the everyday drink, but **Turkish coffee** remains the drink for special moments and guests.

## FAQ

**Where was coffee first drunk?** The plant comes from **Ethiopia**, but the first reliable records of the drink come from **Yemen** in the 15th century.

**Where was the first coffeehouse?** The earliest known coffeehouses appeared in the 16th century in cities such as **Mecca**, **Cairo** and **Damascus**. **Istanbul** followed in 1554 and became one of the key hubs that carried coffeehouse culture to Europe.

**Where does the word "coffee" come from?** From the Arabic **"qahwa"**, via the Turkish "kahve" into Italian ("caffè"), French ("café"), Dutch ("koffie") and English ("coffee").

**Is the Vienna coffee-sacks story true?** Coffee sacks are said to have been found after the siege, but the details are largely **legend**. Vienna's first official coffeehouse license dates from 1685.
