Getting your prices right matters as much as making good coffee. Too cheap, and you lose money on every sale; too expensive, and the tables stay empty. This guide walks you step by step from **cost per cup** to **competitor prices**, **add-on charges**, **menu board layout** and **menu engineering**.

> **Short answer:** First work out the **cost per cup** for every drink. Ingredient cost should stay at **20–30%** of the price before tax; that gives you your **floor price**. Then set the final price by looking at nearby cafés and your own concept. Keep the menu **short**, highlight **high-margin** drinks and review prices **every 3–6 months**.

*Note: Examples use European prices (€). Use the same method with your own local prices and currency.*

## 1. Keep the menu short

A long menu overwhelms customers, bloats your stock and slows your barista down. A good start for a new café:

| Group | Suggestion | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Classics** | Espresso, Americano, cappuccino, latte, flat white | Most of your sales come from here |
| **Filter / specialty** | Filter coffee, Turkish coffee | Filter coffee is fast and high-margin |
| **Cold** | Iced latte, cold brew | A big share of sales in summer |
| **Seasonal** | 1–2 drinks (e.g. pumpkin spice latte in fall) | Rotate as a "drink of the month" |
| **No coffee** | Tea, hot chocolate, chai latte | For friends who don't drink coffee |

**Tip:** Two **sizes** at most (small / large). Seasonal ideas: [winter coffee drinks](/en/winter-coffee-drinks/) and [hot chocolate recipe](/en/hot-chocolate-recipe/).

## 2. Work out your cost per cup

For each drink, add up coffee, milk and cup/lid. Example (coffee €25/kg, milk €1.20/l, rounded):

| Drink | Coffee | Milk | Cup, sugar etc. | **Total cost** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Espresso | 9 g = €0.23 | – | €0.05 | **~€0.28** |
| Cappuccino | 18 g = €0.45 | 120 ml = €0.14 | €0.10 | **~€0.69** |
| Latte | 18 g = €0.45 | 200 ml = €0.24 | €0.10 | **~€0.79** |
| Filter coffee (250 ml) | 15 g = €0.38 | – | €0.10 | **~€0.48** |
| Iced latte | 18 g = €0.45 | 150 ml = €0.18 | €0.25 (cup, lid, ice) | **~€0.88** |
| Turkish coffee | 7 g = €0.14 (€20/kg) | – | €0.15 (water, Turkish delight) | **~€0.29** |

To run the numbers with your own prices, use our [coffee shop cost calculator](/en/coffee-shop-cost-calculator/): it shows cost, gross profit, cost percentage and a suggested price instantly.

## 3. Set the price in three steps

**Step 1 – Floor price (from cost):**

> **Floor price = cost ÷ target cost percentage**, then add tax.

Example: latte costs €0.79, target 25% → 0.79 ÷ 0.25 = **€3.16** before tax. With 19% VAT, **about €3.76**. Go below that and rent and wages get hard to cover.

**Step 2 – Market price (competitors):** Visit **5–10 cafés** near you and note prices and sizes. Chains, neighborhood cafés and specialty cafés usually sit in different price ranges.

**Step 3 – Final price by concept:** Between floor and market price, pick your spot to match your concept:

- **Specialty café:** the top of the market. Tell the story of the beans.
- **Neighborhood café:** the market average. Regulars matter.
- **Kiosk / to-go:** can be a little below average; you win on speed and volume.

**Example (Germany, approx.):**

| Drink | Floor price (25%, incl. VAT) | Typical market price |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Espresso | ~€1.35 | €2.20–2.80 |
| Cappuccino | ~€3.30 | €3.50–4.20 |
| Latte | ~€3.80 | €3.80–4.80 |
| Filter coffee | ~€2.30 | €2.80–3.50 |
| Iced latte | ~€4.20 | €4.20–5.20 |
| Turkish coffee | ~€1.40 | €2.50–3.50 |

**Watch out:** Espresso, filter and Turkish coffee cost little, so **the market** sets their price; they're among your most profitable items. For milk and cold drinks, the floor price is close to the market price, so keep a close eye on costs.

## 4. Add-on charges: small but profitable

| Add-on | Extra cost | Typical charge |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Plant milk (oat, almond)** | ~€0.25–0.35 | +€0.40–0.70 |
| **Extra shot** | ~€0.23 | +€0.50–0.80 |
| **Syrup (10–15 ml)** | ~€0.10 | +€0.40–0.60 |
| **Large size** | ~€0.15–0.30 | +€0.50–0.80 |

These look small but are **high-margin** and give customers a **sense of choice**. More: [coffee syrup guide](/en/coffee-syrup-guide/) and [oat milk for coffee](/en/oat-milk-for-coffee/).

## 5. The menu board: how do customers read it?

- **Skip the currency sign:** "3.80" instead of "€3.80". Many cafés feel the price stands out less that way.
- **Don't line prices up in one column:** Customers then compare prices instead of drinks and pick the cheapest. Put the price right after the name.
- **Highlight high-margin drinks:** a frame, color or "barista's pick" label.
- **Short, clear names:** "Cappuccino" is enough. Explain only where needed, in one line.
- **Readability:** People in the queue must be able to read the board from **3–4 meters (10–13 ft)**.
- **Allergens:** Required in the EU (milk, gluten, nuts and so on), and good practice everywhere.

For board and layout ideas, see [coffee shop design ideas](/en/coffee-shop-design-ideas/).

## 6. Menu engineering: what stays, what goes?

Each month, pull **units sold** and **profit per item** from your POS and sort items into four groups:

| Group | What it means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Star** | Sells well, high margin | Feature it, never cut quality |
| **Plowhorse** | Sells well, low margin | Raise the price in small steps or cut costs |
| **Puzzle** | Sells poorly, high margin | Move it on the board, have baristas recommend it, rename it |
| **Dog** | Sells poorly, low margin | Drop it or rethink it completely |

## 7. Upselling and promotions

- **Combos:** Coffee + cookie or croissant, slightly cheaper than separately. Raises the average ticket.
- **Upselling:** One question is enough: "Would you like a cookie with that?" or "Make it a large?" Friendly, no pressure.
- **Loyalty card:** "10th coffee free" is about a **9% discount** and keeps people coming back.
- **Quiet hours:** A small afternoon discount can fill empty tables.

## 8. When and how to raise prices

- **Track costs:** Note coffee, milk and cup prices every month. If your cost percentage goes **above 30%**, act.
- **Review prices every 3–6 months.**
- **Small steps:** **€0.10–0.20** at a time rather than one big jump.
- **With a new menu:** A price rise paired with a new drink or a fresh board gets noticed less.
- **Keep the quality:** Raising prices and shrinking portions is the fastest way to lose customers.

## Common mistakes

- Pricing **only by competitors** without knowing your own costs.
- Too long a menu: stock, waste and slow service.
- Giving plant milk and syrup away **for free**.
- Never adjusting for years, then one big price jump.
- Hiding your most profitable drinks in a **corner of the board**.

## Checklist

- [ ] I know the **cost per cup** of every drink.
- [ ] I've set my **target cost percentage** (e.g. 25%).
- [ ] I've noted the prices of at least **5 cafés** nearby.
- [ ] My **add-on charges** (plant milk, extra shot, syrup, large) are set.
- [ ] The board is readable from **3–4 meters** and lists allergens.
- [ ] I check sales data monthly and adjust the menu.

## FAQ

**What cost percentage should coffee have?** Usually **20–30%**. Lower for espresso and filter, higher for milk and cold drinks. What counts is the average across the whole menu.

**Should I be cheaper than the competition?** Usually not. A price war wears a small café down fast. Stand out with **quality, speed and friendliness** instead.

**Should to-go be cheaper?** Some cafés charge a little less for to-go since there's no table or dishwashing, but cups and lids are added. Tax rules for eat-in and takeaway differ by country, so check with your accountant.

**How often should I update prices?** Review every 3–6 months, more often if costs are rising fast.
