You see **"specialty coffee"** on café menus and coffee bags everywhere these days. Is it just a marketing term, or genuinely a different kind of coffee? Here's what specialty coffee is, how it differs from regular coffee and how to spot a good one.

> **Short answer:** Specialty coffee is coffee that trained tasters score **80 points or more on a 100-point scale**, with very few defects and a traceable origin. The bag usually lists **country, region, farm, process, roast date and tasting notes**. It costs more, but **brewed fresh and well**, the difference is obvious.

## What does "specialty coffee" mean?

Under the definition commonly used in the industry, green (unroasted) coffee is first checked for **defects**, then roasted and scored by **trained tasters** following a set protocol. Coffees scoring **80 points or more** count as "specialty". The score looks at aroma, acidity, body, sweetness, balance and aftertaste, among other things.

In short, specialty coffee is:

- made from **near-flawless** green coffee,
- **traceable** – the country, region and often the farm are known,
- **carefully processed** and usually **freshly roasted**,
- almost always **Arabica**.

## How it differs from regular coffee

| | Supermarket coffee | Specialty coffee |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Origin info | Often just "blend" | Country, region, often farm |
| Roast | Often dark, flavors flattened | Light to medium, shows origin |
| Date | Best-before date | Roast date |
| Flavor | Roasty, bitter, uniform | Fruity, chocolatey, floral notes |
| Price | Low | Higher |
| Producer | Large roasters | Small roasters, direct trade |

## How to recognize good specialty coffee

1. **Roast date:** Ideally roasted within the last 1–6 weeks. Only a best-before date? Be cautious. See [how to store coffee beans](/en/how-to-store-coffee-beans/).
2. **Origin:** Country and region, ideally farm or co-op – see [coffee growing regions](/en/coffee-growing-regions/).
3. **Process:** Washed, natural or honey.
4. **Altitude and variety:** For example "1,900 m" or names like Heirloom, Bourbon, Geisha.
5. **Tasting notes:** "Blueberry, bergamot, chocolate" – not added flavorings, but the coffee's own taste.
6. **Roast level:** Usually light to medium – see [coffee roast levels](/en/coffee-roast-levels/).

## How to brew specialty coffee

These methods show it off best:

- **[V60](/en/v60-pour-over-guide/)** and **[Chemex](/en/how-to-use-a-chemex/):** for clear, fruity flavors.
- **[AeroPress](/en/how-to-use-an-aeropress/):** easy and forgiving.
- **[French press](/en/how-to-use-a-french-press/):** for more body.
- **[Espresso](/en/home-espresso-machine-guide/):** specialty espresso roasts exist too.

**Grind fresh**, **weigh** your coffee and use **good water**: [grinder guide](/en/best-coffee-grinder/), [coffee scale guide](/en/coffee-scale-guide/), [best water for coffee](/en/best-water-for-coffee/).

## Is specialty coffee worth it?

**Yes, if:** you're curious about flavor, drink your coffee black and can grind it fresh and brew it well.

**Probably not needed, if:** you drink coffee with lots of milk and syrup, or keep pre-ground coffee open for weeks. A good medium-roast supermarket coffee will do.

## FAQ

**Why is specialty coffee more expensive?** Hand-selected cherries, careful processing, small lots and usually fairer prices for farmers.

**Is every "100% Arabica" coffee specialty?** No. Arabica is a prerequisite but not enough on its own – see [Arabica vs Robusta](/en/arabica-vs-robusta/).

**How should I start?** Use a sampler to compare two origins (say, Ethiopia and Colombia) with the same method. It also makes a lovely [gift](/en/coffee-gift-ideas/).
