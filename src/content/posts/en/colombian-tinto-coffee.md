Early morning in Bogotá. At a crowded bus stop, a man with two big thermoses calls out: *"¡Tinto, tinto!"* For a few coins he fills a small plastic cup with dark, hot, sweetened coffee. It's drunk before the bus arrives, the cup is tossed, and the day begins.

This is Colombia's everyday coffee: **tinto**.

## What does tinto mean?

In Spanish, *tinto* means "dyed" or "dark"; in Spain it usually means red wine. But ask for "un tinto" in Colombia and you won't get wine. You'll get **a small cup of plain black coffee**.

A tinto is usually:
- **small** (a bit bigger than an espresso cup),
- **black, no milk**,
- often **sweetened** with sugar or **panela**,
- and **cheap**, available at any hour on almost every corner.

## The street coffee sellers: tinteros

In Colombian cities, people who sell coffee from thermoses are called **tinteros**. You'll find them at bus stops, in markets and outside offices. Many run a small family business: the coffee is brewed at home in the morning, poured into thermoses and sold all day. Over a tinto, conversations start, deals are made and friends say hello.

## The paradox: the best coffee leaves the country

Colombia is one of the world's largest coffee producers, known for growing only **Arabica**. Yet for decades **the best beans were exported**, while coffee drunk at home was often made from lower grades. That's one reason tinto is often dark, a little bitter and sweetened.

That's changing: specialty cafés have opened in Bogotá and Medellín, and Colombians are discovering their own best coffee. More on that: [what is specialty coffee](/en/what-is-specialty-coffee/).

## The coffee region and Juan Valdez

Colombia's **coffee region** in the Andes (the *Eje Cafetero*, around Caldas, Quindío and Risaralda), with its green slopes, colorful towns and small family farms, was added to the **UNESCO World Heritage List in 2011** as the "Coffee Cultural Landscape of Colombia".

And there's a famous character: **Juan Valdez**. The mustached coffee farmer with his hat and his mule Conchita was created in 1958 for the Colombian Coffee Growers Federation's advertising, and he's still the face of Colombian coffee.

Colombian coffee is usually described as **balanced, caramel-sweet and lightly fruity**. How it compares: [coffee growing regions](/en/coffee-growing-regions/).

## The coffee of this story: tinto at home

**Classic tinto (2 small cups):**

1. **20 g** medium-ground Colombian coffee and **300 ml (10 oz)** hot water (boiled, then rested 30 seconds).
2. Pour slowly through a cloth or paper filter; a [V60](/en/v60-pour-over-guide/) or [drip coffee maker](/en/drip-coffee-maker-guide/) works too.
3. Pour into small cups and sweeten if you like.

**Country-style tinto with panela:**

1. In a cezve or small pot, bring **300 ml (10 oz) water** and **1–2 tbsp panela** (or brown sugar) to a boil until the panela dissolves.
2. Take it off the heat and stir in **2 tbsp** medium-ground coffee.
3. Let it steep **4 minutes** so the grounds settle.
4. Strain through a fine sieve or filter into cups.

Panela is unrefined sugar made by boiling down sugarcane juice and pressing it into blocks; it tastes of caramel and molasses. Brown sugar or a little molasses can stand in.

## FAQ

**Is tinto an espresso?** No. Tinto is usually filtered or boiled black coffee, lighter than espresso but served in small cups.

**What's coffee with milk called in Colombia?** Usually *café con leche*; with just a splash of milk it's a *perico*.

**What is panela?** Unrefined block sugar made from sugarcane juice, used widely in Colombian cooking.
