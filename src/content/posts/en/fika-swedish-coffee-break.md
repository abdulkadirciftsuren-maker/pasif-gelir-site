An office in Stockholm, 3 p.m. Meetings pause and nobody's looking at a screen. A thermos of coffee and a plate of cinnamon buns arrive in the kitchen, and everyone gathers around the table. It's not a celebration, just an ordinary day. In Swedish, it's called **fika**.

## What is fika?

Fika is **a break with coffee and something sweet**, but it's more than that. In Swedish it's both a noun and a verb: "Ska vi fika?", "Shall we have fika?" Meeting a friend, chatting with colleagues, ten quiet minutes alone by the window: all of it can be fika.

What matters is **not the coffee but the pause**. You don't rush fika, you don't check your phone and you don't drink it standing up.

## The curious origin of the word

"Fika" is said to come from **"kaffi"**, an old Swedish word for coffee, with **the syllables swapped**: kaf-fi → fi-ka. Syllable-flipping slang like this was popular in the 19th century. Today the word is a symbol of Swedish culture.

## The country that banned coffee

Sweden's love for coffee wasn't always so warm. In the **18th century**, coffee was heavily taxed and even **banned** several times; the state saw it as a harmful luxury. King Gustav III is said to have made two twin convicts drink coffee and tea every day to prove coffee was dangerous, though how much of that story is true is debated. In the end, coffee won: Sweden is now among the countries with the **highest coffee consumption per person**.

## Fika at work

Many Swedish workplaces have one or two **set fika times** a day, often around 10 a.m. and 3 p.m. The boss and the new intern sit at the same table. Many Swedes say team spirit and good ideas are born not in meetings but over fika.

## Cinnamon buns and "påtår"

The star of fika is the **kanelbulle**, a **cinnamon bun with cardamom in the dough**. Sweden celebrates **Cinnamon Bun Day** (Kanelbullens dag) on October 4. The coffee is usually **light to medium roast drip coffee**, and lots of it. Even the second cup has a name: **påtår** ("refill").

Far north in Lapland lives the tradition of **kaffeost**, little cubes of cheese in coffee, but that's another story.

## Does fika exist elsewhere?

In Turkey the idea is familiar: **tea time**, or **Turkish coffee with Turkish delight** for guests. A Turkish proverb says, "A cup of coffee is remembered for forty years." Fika feels the same: **the coffee is the excuse; being together is the point.**

## The coffee of this story: Swedish-style fika at home

**Coffee:** Plenty of **drip coffee** from light to medium roast beans, about **55–60 g per liter** (roughly 2 oz per quart). Choosing a machine: [drip coffee maker guide](/en/drip-coffee-maker-guide/); better drip coffee: [better drip coffee tips](/en/better-drip-coffee-tips/).

**Easy cinnamon buns (kanelbullar, about 20):**

- **Dough:** 500 g (4 cups) flour, 7 g (1 packet) dry yeast, 250 ml (1 cup) lukewarm milk, 75 g (⅓ cup) soft butter, 75 g (⅓ cup) sugar, 1 tsp ground cardamom, a pinch of salt
- **Filling:** 100 g (7 tbsp) soft butter, 75 g (⅓ cup) sugar, 1 tbsp cinnamon
- **Topping:** 1 egg, pearl sugar

1. Knead the dough ingredients, cover and let rise for **1 hour**.
2. Roll into a rectangle, spread the filling, roll up and cut into 20 pieces.
3. Place in paper cups or on a tray and let rest **30 minutes**.
4. Brush with egg, sprinkle with pearl sugar and bake at **220 °C (425 °F) for 8–10 minutes**.

**Tip:** Cardamom suits both the bun and the coffee; **Turkish coffee with cardamom** makes a lovely fika too: [how to make Turkish coffee](/en/how-to-make-turkish-coffee/).

Roast levels explained: [coffee roast levels](/en/coffee-roast-levels/). Another coffee tradition: [caffè sospeso](/en/caffe-sospeso-suspended-coffee/).

## FAQ

**How long does fika last?** There's no rule; at work usually 15–20 minutes, with friends it can last hours.

**Does it have to be coffee?** Usually, but tea or juice are fine too. The break and the conversation are what count.

**How do Swedes drink coffee?** Mostly light to medium roast drip coffee, plenty of it, black or with a little milk.
