A cup of coffee is about **98% water**. Even with the best beans and the best machine, your coffee will be mediocre if the water isn't right. The good news: finding the right water is easy. Here we compare tap, filtered and bottled water and explain how hardness and chlorine affect your cup.

> **Short answer:** Use **odor-free, good-tasting water of medium hardness**. If your tap water is very hard or smells of chlorine, use a **filter pitcher** or a **low-mineral still bottled water**. **Distilled water** and **highly mineralized mineral water** aren't suitable for coffee. For Turkish coffee, always use **fresh, cold water**.

## How water affects coffee

Water dissolves the flavor compounds in coffee, and the **minerals** in it – mainly calcium and magnesium – help:

- **Very hard water:** Coffee tastes **flat and dull**; the brightness disappears. Machines **scale up** quickly.
- **Very soft water:** Coffee tastes **sour, thin and unbalanced**. Some machine sensors don't work reliably with mineral-free water.
- **Medium hardness:** Brings out flavors in the best balance.
- **Chlorine:** A chlorine smell from the tap comes through in the cup.

Coffee experts generally recommend water with about **75–250 mg of dissolved minerals per liter** (ideally around 150 mg/L, often shown as **TDS** on bottled water labels or water reports).

## Water options compared

| Water | For coffee | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tap water** | Depends on where you live | Fine if it tastes and smells good |
| **Filter pitcher** | Usually good | Less chlorine, slightly less hardness |
| **Low-mineral still bottled water** | Very good | Check the label |
| **Sparkling or highly mineralized water** | Not suitable | Too many minerals, salty or metallic taste |
| **Distilled water** | Not on its own | Coffee turns sour and flat |
| **Reverse osmosis** | Weak on its own | Removes most minerals; remineralizing systems are better |

## Finding your water hardness

Hardness varies hugely from place to place. Your **local water utility's quality report** lists it, or you can use **test strips**. If your water is hard, a filter helps both the taste and your machine.

## Water for Turkish coffee

- Use **fresh, cold water** – cold water in the cezve matters for the foam.
- Don't reuse water that has already been boiled and cooled; it can taste flatter.
- More in [how to make Turkish coffee](/en/how-to-make-turkish-coffee/) and the [Turkish coffee machine guide](/en/turkish-coffee-machine-guide/).

## The right temperature

Temperature matters as much as the water itself: about **92–96 °C (198–205 °F)** is ideal for pour-over and espresso. Wait 30–60 seconds after boiling. A temperature-control [gooseneck kettle](/en/gooseneck-kettle-guide/) makes it easy. Too hot tastes bitter, too cool tastes sour – see [why is my coffee bitter or sour?](/en/why-is-my-coffee-bitter/).

## Machines and scale

- Hard water scales up machines fast. Scale stops the water from getting hot enough and spoils the taste.
- Measure hardness with **test strips** and set your machine's hardness setting to match.
- Use the **water filter recommended by the manufacturer** in super-automatics and espresso machines.
- Descale regularly: [how to descale a coffee maker](/en/how-to-descale-a-coffee-maker/).

## FAQ

**What's the best water for coffee?** Odor-free, good-tasting water with medium mineral content. Filtered tap water is the easiest option for most people.

**Can I use mineral water?** Still, low-mineral water, yes. Sparkling or heavily mineralized water ruins the flavor.

**Can I reboil water in the kettle?** It's generally not a health issue, but fresh water tastes better. Your kettle scales up too – clean it regularly.
