A rainy afternoon in Vienna. You push open the door: velvet banquettes, marble tables, bentwood chairs, the yellow glow of chandeliers. A waiter in a black vest brings **a cup of coffee and a glass of water** on a silver tray. You take a newspaper from its wooden holder. Nobody asks if you'd like anything else, nobody brings the bill unasked. You could stay **until evening**.

This is the **Viennese coffee house**, the Wiener Kaffeehaus.

## A place that sells time, not coffee

The writer Stefan Zweig described the Viennese coffee house as a kind of democratic club that offers everything for the price of a cup of coffee. What's really on sale here is **time and space**. Reading, writing, playing chess or just watching people for hours over one cup is perfectly normal.

The culture is so special that in **2011 it was added to Austria's national inventory of UNESCO intangible cultural heritage**. How coffee reached Vienna, and who opened the first coffee house, is covered in our [history of coffee](/en/history-of-coffee/).

## The unwritten rules

- **Water always comes:** Coffee is served with a glass of water, and a good waiter refills it without asking.
- **The "Herr Ober":** often in a black vest and bow tie, a little grumpy but highly professional.
- **Free newspapers:** Dozens of papers and magazines on wooden holders travel from table to table.
- **No rush:** Nobody moves you along; you ask for the bill when you're ready.
- **Marble tables and Thonet chairs:** The famous bentwood chairs are the symbol of these rooms.

## Literary salons

Around 1900, Vienna's coffee houses were a "second home" for writers, artists and thinkers. The poet **Peter Altenberg** is said to have sat in **Café Central** almost every day; today a figure of him greets you at the entrance. There are stories of revolutionaries playing chess and authors who never left their regular table. This writing even has its own name: **Kaffeehausliteratur**, coffee house literature.

Today, **Café Central**, **Café Landtmann**, **Café Sperl** and **Café Hawelka** draw locals and visitors alike.

## What's on the menu?

In Vienna, "a coffee, please" isn't enough; coffee has dozens of names:

| Name | What is it? |
| --- | --- |
| **Wiener Melange** | Espresso + hot milk + milk foam (like a cappuccino, a bit milder) |
| **Einspänner** | Black coffee in a glass topped with lots of whipped cream |
| **Kleiner / Großer Brauner** | Small or large black coffee with a little milk or cream |
| **Verlängerter** | Espresso lengthened with water (similar to an Americano) |
| **Kleiner Schwarzer** | A short black espresso |

The classic companions: **Sachertorte** or **apple strudel**.

## The coffee of this story: Wiener Melange at home

**For 1 cup:**

1. Make an **espresso** (about 30–40 ml / 1–1.3 oz). No machine? Strong **moka pot** coffee works too: [how to make espresso](/en/how-to-make-espresso/), [how to use a moka pot](/en/how-to-use-a-moka-pot/).
2. Heat **120–150 ml (4–5 oz) milk** to 140–150 °F (60–65 °C) and froth it softly: [milk frother guide](/en/milk-frother-guide/) or [how to froth milk without a frother](/en/how-to-froth-milk-without-a-frother/).
3. Pour the milk over the coffee and spoon a little foam on top.
4. Serve with a glass of water, ideally next to a slice of cake.

**Einspänner:** Pour strong black coffee into a glass, top with plenty of **unsweetened whipped cream** and don't stir: the hot coffee comes through the cream.

## FAQ

**How long can I stay in a Viennese coffee house?** As long as you like. Sitting for hours over one cup is part of the tradition.

**Is a Wiener Melange the same as a cappuccino?** Very similar; a melange is usually a little milder and milkier, sometimes topped with cream.

**Should I tip?** In Austria it's customary to round up by about 5–10%.
