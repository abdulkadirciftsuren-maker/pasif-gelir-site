An office in Helsinki, 2 p.m. Without anyone saying a word, everyone stands up; a big thermos of coffee is waiting in the kitchen. Pale brown coffee pours into the cups, with a slice of cardamom sweet bread on the side. Fifteen minutes later everyone is back at their desks, and before evening the scene will repeat.

Welcome to **the country that drinks the most coffee per person**: Finland.

## A record-breaking love

In international statistics, Finland has topped **per-capita coffee consumption** for years; many sources put it at roughly **10–12 kg (22–26 lb)** per adult per year. That's about **4–5 cups a day**. Norway, Denmark and Sweden sit near the top too.

Why so much? The usual explanations: long, dark, cold winters; coffee being part of every social occasion; and the coffee break being almost a rule of working life.

## The coffee break: kahvitauko

In Finnish, a coffee break is a **kahvitauko**. In many industries, collective agreements provide **one or two short breaks** a day, and everyone simply calls them coffee breaks. Coffee is built into the working day.

It's not just about caffeine: in Finnish offices, these breaks are moments when hierarchy softens and everyone sits at the same table, much like Sweden's [fika](/en/fika-swedish-coffee-break/).

## A love of light roasts

Finnish coffee is nothing like the dark espresso of the south. Traditionally it's **very lightly roasted**, brewed as drip coffee and drunk in quantity. It tastes milder, a little bright and fruity. One explanation often given is that Finns used to roast at home, and light roasts suited the soft northern water.

In Finnish supermarkets, **numbers from 1 to 4** on the bags show the roast level; light and medium roasts usually sell best. More on roasts: [coffee roast levels](/en/coffee-roast-levels/).

## Coffee with cheese: kahvijuusto

A curious tradition from northern Finland and Lapland: **kahvijuusto**, "coffee cheese". A mild cheese, lightly browned in the oven, is cut into cubes, placed **in the cup** and topped with hot coffee. The cheese softens and is eaten with a spoon, and the coffee takes on a slightly salty, creamy taste. More on salt and coffee: [salt in coffee](/en/salt-in-coffee/).

## The seven-cookie coffee table

A classic of Finnish hospitality: **"seitsemän sortin kahvipöytä"**, the seven-kinds coffee table. By old tradition, a good host offered **seven different treats** with coffee: first the cardamom sweet bread **pulla**, then cookies, and cake last. Seven is rare today, but a generous coffee table is still part of birthdays, name days and funerals.

It's familiar from Turkey too, where coffee comes with Turkish delight, chocolate or cookies. Serving Turkish coffee: [Turkish coffee cups](/en/turkish-coffee-cups/).

## The coffee of this story: Finnish-style coffee at home

**Coffee:** Grind light-roast beans medium and brew in a [drip coffee maker](/en/drip-coffee-maker-guide/) or a [V60](/en/v60-pour-over-guide/). About **55–60 g per liter** (roughly 2 oz per quart) is a good start. Better drip coffee: [better drip coffee tips](/en/better-drip-coffee-tips/).

**Try a kahvijuusto:** Cut a lightly salty cheese that doesn't melt when heated (halloumi works) into small cubes and brown them lightly on both sides in a dry pan. Drop a few cubes into a cup and pour hot drip coffee over them. Drink the coffee first, then spoon up the softened cheese.

**On the side:** A cardamom sweet bun. A similar recipe is in our Sweden story: [fika and cinnamon buns](/en/fika-swedish-coffee-break/).

## FAQ

**Do Finns really drink the most coffee in the world?** Per person, they're at or near the top of most statistics. The ranking shifts a little by source and year, but Finland is almost always first.

**Is the coffee break required by law in Finland?** It comes less from a law than from collective agreements in many industries. In daily life, these breaks are simply called coffee breaks.

**What does Finnish coffee taste like?** Thanks to the light roast: mild, smooth and slightly bright, with little bitterness.
