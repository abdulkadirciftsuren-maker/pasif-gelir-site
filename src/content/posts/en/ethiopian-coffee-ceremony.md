A living room in Addis Ababa. Fresh grass is scattered on the floor and incense burns in the corner. The host sits on a low stool, shaking **raw green coffee beans** in an iron pan over charcoal. When the beans crackle and darken, she carries the pan to her guests, and everyone waves the smoke toward their faces. The ceremony has only just begun, and it will last at least an hour.

This is Ethiopia's **coffee ceremony**, called **buna** in Amharic.

## In coffee's homeland

Ethiopia is considered **the birthplace of Arabica coffee**; in the forests of the Kaffa region, coffee still grows wild. The legend of Kaldi the goatherd comes from here too (see [history of coffee](/en/history-of-coffee/)). Ethiopians drink a large share of their own coffee, about half by many accounts. An Amharic saying sums it up: **"Buna dabo naw"**, "Coffee is our bread."

## The ceremony, step by step

1. **Preparation:** Fresh grass, sometimes flowers, on the floor; incense (usually frankincense) burning; small handleless cups lined up on a tray.
2. **Washing and roasting:** The green beans are washed and roasted in a flat pan over charcoal, **right in front of the guests**.
3. **Aroma:** The roasting smoke is wafted toward the guests; praising the aroma is good manners.
4. **Grinding:** The beans are crushed by hand in a mortar.
5. **Boiling:** The coffee boils with water in the **jebena**, a round-bellied clay pot with a long neck.
6. **Serving:** A tuft of fiber is sometimes tucked into the spout as a filter, and the coffee is poured **from a height in one stream**, without stirring up the grounds.

It's served with **popcorn** or roasted barley (kolo). Most people take it **with sugar**; in some regions **with salt** or butter. More on that: [salt in coffee](/en/salt-in-coffee/).

## Three rounds: abol, tona, bereka

The same grounds are brewed **three times**, and each round has a name:

| Round | Name | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st | **Abol** | The darkest, strongest coffee |
| 2nd | **Tona** | A gentler second round |
| 3rd | **Bereka** | "Blessing", the blessed final round |

The third cup is said to bring good wishes to those who drink it, so leaving **before the third round** isn't considered very polite.

## More than coffee

The ceremony can happen once or several times a day. Neighbors drop in, family news is shared, disputes are settled, guests are welcomed. **Coffee is a way of building relationships**, much like Sweden's [fika](/en/fika-swedish-coffee-break/).

## The coffee of this story: Ethiopian coffee at home

A full ceremony at home is a big undertaking, but capturing the taste is easy.

**1. The modern way: V60 with Ethiopian beans.** Ethiopian coffees (Yirgacheffe, Sidamo, Guji) are famous for **floral, fruity** flavors that shine with a **light roast** and **pour-over** brewing. Guide: [V60 pour-over guide](/en/v60-pour-over-guide/). Origins: [coffee growing regions](/en/coffee-growing-regions/).

**2. Jebena-style, with a cezve:**

- For one cup (about 80 ml / 3 oz of water), use **8–10 g** of medium-fine coffee (a bit coarser than Turkish).
- Put coffee and water in a small [cezve](/en/how-to-choose-a-cezve/), heat gently until just before boiling, then take it off the heat.
- Let it rest **2–3 minutes** so the grounds settle.
- Pour **slowly, in one go**, leaving the grounds in the pot.
- Add sugar or a pinch of salt if you like; popcorn on the side is perfect.

Unlike Turkish coffee, the grind is coarser and the goal is a **clear cup**, not foam. For Turkish coffee: [how to make Turkish coffee](/en/how-to-make-turkish-coffee/).

## FAQ

**What does buna mean?** It's Amharic for "coffee". The ceremony is often simply called buna.

**What is a jebena?** A traditional Ethiopian coffee pot, usually black clay, with a round belly and a long neck.

**How long does the ceremony take?** From roasting to the third round, usually **1–2 hours**.

**What does Ethiopian coffee taste like?** It depends on the region; many are known for floral, lemony or berry-like notes.
