The heart or tulip on top of a coffee doesn't just look pretty: it shows you've made **a good espresso** and **silky milk foam**. The good news: latte art isn't a talent, it's **technique**. This guide walks you through **how to steam the milk**, **how to pour** and how to make a **heart and a tulip**, step by step.

> **Short answer:** Latte art takes three things: a **fresh espresso with crema**, **glossy microfoam that flows like paint** (140–150 °F / 60–65 °C) and the right pour: **start high and thin**, and once the cup is half full, **bring the pitcher close to the surface** and draw the shape. Start with the **heart**.

## What you need

- **An espresso machine with a steam wand:** The easiest way. See our [home espresso machine guide](/en/home-espresso-machine-guide/).
- **A milk pitcher:** 12 oz (350 ml) with a **pointed spout**. See [espresso accessories](/en/espresso-accessories/).
- **A wide cup:** A 6–8 oz (180–250 ml) cappuccino cup leaves room for the pattern.
- **Cold milk:** Straight from the fridge. Cold milk gives you more time to steam.

**Which milk?** **Whole milk** is the easiest: protein holds the foam and fat makes it silky. For plant milk, choose a **"barista"** oat milk. See [oat milk for coffee](/en/oat-milk-for-coffee/).

## 1. Espresso: get the base right

Espresso is the canvas. You need **freshly roasted** beans and a **thick brown crema**. With weak crema or stale coffee, the pattern falls apart fast. A typical start: **18 g coffee → 36 g espresso** in 25–30 seconds.

## 2. Microfoam: 80% of the job

The goal: milk with **tiny, invisible bubbles**, **glossy** and **flowing like paint**. Not stiff, thick foam.

1. Fill the pitcher **one third** with cold milk (~5 oz / 150 ml for one cup).
2. **Purge the steam wand:** Open it for a second to clear the water.
3. **Place the tip just below the milk surface**, with the pitcher slightly tilted.
4. **Stretch (add air):** Open the steam fully; you should hear a **"tss tss"** sound, like paper tearing. Keep going until the volume grows by **20–30%** and the pitcher feels **lukewarm** (~100 °F / 37 °C).
5. **Make a whirlpool:** Lower the tip a little so the milk **spins**. The sound quiets down and big bubbles disappear.
6. **Stop at 140–150 °F (60–65 °C):** When the pitcher is **"very hot, hard to hold"**. Above 160 °F (70 °C), milk loses its sweetness and tastes cooked.
7. **Clean right away:** Wipe the wand with a damp cloth and purge it.
8. **Polish:** **Tap** the pitcher lightly on the counter (pops big bubbles) and **swirl** the milk. The surface should **shine like wet paint**.

**Can I use a milk frother?** Electric frothers usually make foam that's too stiff; a simple heart is possible but hard. Without a machine: [how to froth milk without a frother](/en/how-to-froth-milk-without-a-frother/).

## 3. Pouring basics

1. **Hold and tilt the cup:** About **45°**, so the espresso pools on one side.
2. **Base:** Hold the pitcher **about 4 inches (10 cm) high** and pour a **thin stream** into the middle. The milk dives **under** the crema and the surface stays brown.
3. **Once the cup is half full:** Bring the pitcher **right down to the surface** (spout almost touching the coffee) and **speed up** the flow a little. A white spot appears: this is where the pattern starts.
4. **Slowly level the cup** as it fills and the pattern spreads.

## 4. The heart: your first pattern

1. Pour the base (high, thin stream).
2. When the cup is **half full**, bring the pitcher close and pour **steadily into the center**. The white circle should **grow**.
3. When the cup is **almost full**, **lift** the pitcher, **thin** the stream and draw one line **forward** through the circle.
4. The line pulls the circle down into the **point of the heart**.

**Tip:** If the circle won't grow, the pitcher isn't close enough or the foam is too thin. If it's messy, the foam is too thick.

## 5. The tulip: one step further

A tulip is **2–3 small circles** pushed into each other.

1. Pour the base.
2. Bring the pitcher close, pour a **small circle** and **stop** (cut the stream, lift the pitcher).
3. Pour the second circle a little **further back**, **pushing forward** slightly as you pour so it nudges the first one ahead.
4. Repeat for the third circle.
5. Finally, lift the pitcher and **cut through** the circles with a thin stream.

Once the tulip feels easy, the next step is the **rosetta** (leaf): wiggle the pitcher side to side while moving back, then cut through.

## Common mistakes

| Problem | Likely cause | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Big bubbles, stiff foam | Too much air, tip too far out | Stretch for less time, lower the tip a little |
| No white pattern appears | Pitcher too high or foam too thin | Bring the pitcher right down, add a bit more air |
| Pattern falls apart at once | Weak crema, stale coffee, separated milk | Use fresh beans, pour the milk right away |
| Lopsided heart | Leveling the cup too early or cutting at an angle | Pour into the center, cut straight forward |
| Milk tastes cooked | Went above 160 °F (70 °C) | Stop at 140–150 °F, use a thermometer |
| Foam separates in the pitcher | Waited too long | Swirl and pour right after steaming |

## Practice without wasting milk

- **Water + a drop of dish soap:** Steamed, it makes a milk-like foam, so you can practice your **steaming technique** without milk (don't drink it, practice only!).
- **2–3 tries a day:** Short and frequent beats long and rare.
- **Film yourself:** Record your pour on your phone to see pitcher height and speed.
- **Change one thing at a time:** Fix the foam first, then the pour.

## FAQ

**How long does it take to learn latte art?** Most people pour a decent heart within **a few weeks** of regular practice. Tulips and rosettas take a bit longer.

**Does latte art taste better?** The pattern itself doesn't, but milk that's good enough for latte art is **silky and sweet**, so the drink tastes better.

**Which drink is it for?** Easiest in a **flat white** or **cappuccino**, and it works in a latte too. Differences: [latte vs cappuccino vs flat white](/en/latte-vs-cappuccino-vs-flat-white/).

**Does it work with plant milk?** Yes, with **barista** oat milks. Add a little less air and stop at 130–140 °F (55–60 °C); plant milks can split at high heat.
