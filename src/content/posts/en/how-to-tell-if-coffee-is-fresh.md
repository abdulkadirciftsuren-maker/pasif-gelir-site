**Freshness** is one of the biggest factors in how coffee tastes. Even the most expensive beans taste flat when they're stale. So how can you tell if coffee is fresh? Here's how to read the dates on the bag and a few simple tests you can do at home.

> **Short answer:** Look for a **roast date**. Beans usually taste best from **a few days to 4–6 weeks** after roasting. If the bag only has a **best-before date**, you can't tell when it was roasted. At home, check freshness with **smell**, **bloom** and **crema or foam**.

## What do the dates on the bag mean?

| Date | What it tells you | Useful for freshness? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Roast date** | The day the coffee was roasted | The most useful date |
| **Best-before date** | Usually one to two years after roasting | Doesn't tell you when it was roasted |
| **Packing date** | The day it was packed | May or may not match the roast date |

Supermarket coffee usually only shows a best-before date. Roasters and specialty brands print the roast date – see [what is specialty coffee](/en/what-is-specialty-coffee/).

## When does coffee taste best?

Freshly roasted beans release a lot of **CO₂** in the first days, which can make brewing uneven. That's why coffee benefits from a short **rest**:

| Method | Best window after roasting (approx.) |
| --- | --- |
| Pour-over, V60, Chemex, French press | From 3–4 days, for about 4–6 weeks |
| Espresso | From 7–10 days, for about 4–6 weeks |
| Turkish coffee (pre-ground) | Within 2–3 weeks of grinding |

These are guidelines – they depend on the coffee and how you store it. More in [how to store coffee beans](/en/how-to-store-coffee-beans/).

## 4 simple freshness tests

1. **Smell test:** Opening the bag should release a strong, pleasant coffee aroma. Weak or cardboard-like? It's going stale.
2. **Bloom test:** On the first pour in a V60 or Chemex, fresh coffee swells up and bubbles. Stale coffee barely blooms – see the [V60 guide](/en/v60-pour-over-guide/).
3. **Crema test:** A thick, lasting crema on espresso signals freshness. Stale coffee gives thin, pale crema – see the [home espresso machine guide](/en/home-espresso-machine-guide/).
4. **Foam test (Turkish coffee):** Fresh Turkish coffee makes plenty of stable foam. Little foam usually means stale – see [how to make Turkish coffee](/en/how-to-make-turkish-coffee/).

## 6 tips for buying fresh coffee

1. **Check the roast date** – ideally roasted within the last 1–4 weeks.
2. **Buy whole beans** and grind right before brewing – see the [grinder guide](/en/best-coffee-grinder/).
3. **Buy small bags:** what you'll use in 2–4 weeks.
4. **Choose bags with a one-way valve.**
5. **Buy where turnover is high:** For pre-ground Turkish coffee, small packs from busy shops are best – see [how to buy Turkish coffee](/en/how-to-buy-turkish-coffee/).
6. **Online:** Favor products whose reviews mention freshness.

## Can stale coffee be saved?

Not completely, but you can improve it:

- **Grind a little finer** and use **a little more coffee**.
- Drink it **with milk** – it masks stale notes. See the [milk frother guide](/en/milk-frother-guide/).
- Try **[cold brew](/en/how-to-make-cold-brew/)** – older beans show fewer flaws that way.

## FAQ

**Is coffee best right after roasting?** Usually not. In the first 1–3 days there's still a lot of gas in the beans and the flavor can be unbalanced.

**Is expired coffee harmful?** Generally not, but it tastes noticeably weaker. If there's mold or a musty smell, don't use it.

**Are oily beans stale?** Oils are normal on dark roasts. A very oily light roast may be old – see [coffee roast levels](/en/coffee-roast-levels/).
